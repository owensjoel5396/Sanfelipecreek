Evolution of the Book Cover for San Felipe Creek

We're going to talk about AI here, so tighten up your wig.

AI is a terrible and wonderful thing at the same time. Terrible because it gets abused, and the traditional writing community has taken a hard stance against it. Wonderful because the use of AI as an editor, and as an image generator is unparalleled. And I'll share my thoughts with you. Substack currently utilizes a program to detect AI-generated posts. It has been proven that the addition of a period can change it's results from "99% Human" to "AI generated", and that a phrase like "He took her hand," versus "He slipped his hand in hers," can set off an alarm. But as to which one sets it off is a matter of surrounding text. So I think it amounts to a generous pile of asteroid ash. (Can you tell I wrote that?)

For me, creating the first book cover was something I couldn't afford to pay someone to do. It was in the thousands of dollars to hire an artist. Believe me, I'd get the airconditioning fixed on my truck, first. I'm not wealthy. I was already using ChatGPT to send my paragraphs and chapters into so they could be checked for spelling, smoothing, and to catch as much of my poor punctuation as it could. It became early entertainment for me to take my chapters, feed it into the AI with the query "Please illustrate this for me. My characters in are junior high, and have this kind of hair, are barefoot, etc." It made some great things for me to take into Corel PhotoPaint, resize to 4" by 6", print and stick in cheap picture frames to adorn my writing table. And sometimes, it could be really, really funny. For instance:


So, not only did poor Michael look like he was going to stumble, but he was going to fall on a 25-28 year-old Trish who apparently was still enrolled at DRJH. Keep in mind, the text is something I overlaid in Corel PhotoPaint, eye-dropping the color from the sky. AI imagined my characters from the portion of the book I had started. And they are sitting on the wrong side of the creek. In Del Rio at Lions Park, you couldn't sit on that side in the 1970s; it was a wild jungle of thorny bushes there. If you are looking from the east, it still doesn't work because the dam would be right behind them. But, I digress.

Getting help from ChatGPT was such an easy decision about an hour later, I bought a subscription to the service&mdasha rare thing for me. I had already used it at work to help me with "I have this long, long, technical thing I am trying to discuss with another engineer across the country. Can you take my mess and simplify it?" and watch as it took two pages of asteroid ash, and turn it into two working paragraphs that I edited lightly for taste, and sent it on its way. They never said "I sure wish you had sent me the two page explanation instead of having an AI reduce this from TL;DR to completely readable and short." I think we have to use the tools at hand, and use them wisely.

I look at it like this. I could hire a Merkel High School English teacher to sit for hours, looking over my shoulder. I could type a paragraph and ask, "How does this sound? Does it get my meaning across?" He or she would say, "I think I'd reword this sentence and remove the extra comma and adjective." Better yet, why don't you remove these two paragraphs of he did this, then he did that, then he did something, looked and smiled, and change it into "Taking a bite, he smiled." There is not one person who would have said "The English teacher should be listed as the person who wrote your book." That would be "Doubleplus ridiculous, bordering on thought-crime." Or I could pay $8.00 per month for a tool that does this, works whatever hours I have available, even overnight, without griping, needing to be fed, or taking a restroom break, and has time to take care of grading their own papers.

So, there are some who will pursue AI as an editing tool with their machete.
But technology has always done this.
I own slide-rules, but calculators abound. Most teachers I show slide-rules to have no idea what they are, much less how they work.
Typesetters were displaced by desktop publishing.
Film processing was displaced by digital photography.
Drafting departments went from drawing boards to CAD. (I took pencil drafting courses. I've used CAD since 1997.)
Darkrooms disappeared. (We had one that I used when I was in high school. Two friends had their own makeshift darkrooms at home.)
Linotype operators disappeared.
Telephone operators disappeared.
And somebody in every one of those transitions had a perfectly understandable argument for why the old method represented real craftsmanship.

A person can still be a wonderful photographer without developing their own film.
A designer can still be an amazing designer without manually drawing every Bézier curve. (But I own the manual tools to do that on paper, and I can also do it on several pieces of software.)
A person doesn't stop being an author because a machine helps them identify a clumsy sentence, any more than they cease to be an author because Microsoft Word has an automatic spell-checker.

I've spent my entire life learning the technology. I didn't look at the Z80 microprocessor and say "Real computers use vacuum-tubes."
I didn't look at a PAL and say, "A proper decoder should be built from individual glue-logic chips."

AI doesn't supply taste. It doesn't generate my memories of life in Del Rio, Texas. It doesn't have a clue what details matter and which ones don't. It doesn't know when a scene feels emotionally false.
That's what I do. No one has to like it. But I don't have to pretend that the tool isn't extraordinarily useful.
