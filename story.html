San Felipe Creek - Background behind this Story

Hi, I'm Joel Owens. Ordinary guy. Decided one cool February evening to write a book.

I think a lot of people remember the highlights of their younger years, and they remember them fondly.

Over time, we tend to wash out the bad and leave only the good. Maybe we just exaggerate the good, and that's where we come up with sayings like "The good ol' days."

Probably some wisdom could be learned from Carly Simon's "Anticipation," where she reminds us that these are the good old days. Often, we don't recognize them while we are living them.

It's around the age of Junior High that I, and others I have visited with, experienced what they felt in their hearts was their first love. And those are powerful emotions at an age where emotions carry a lot of weight. Of course, very few of those first-loves ever actually pan out for either person. Time and distance come to carry us away. We may get to know the other person and realize they aren't quite the dream-like fit we imagined them to be, and we go separate ways. But the memories tied to those emotions are hard to shake.

For some, we use time to polish them into something happy that we can enjoy remembering.

Sometimes those memories can bring back a lot of hurt and even some damage to our sense of self-confidence. I imagine them sometimes in the way we think of Charlie Brown with a small cloud hovering over him, raining just on us.

As a result, the interactions we have during those times, especially those of a close friendship and maybe more than that, can play a larger part of forming us than we like to acknowledge.

As for me, some of the less happy of those memories left me feeling discarded and replaced by 'the better guy'. I went the path of comparing myself in a negative way to other people around me, and I was doing it with a measuring stick that cut unfairly. I don't think I was the only person who did this, but I felt very much alone in that world. It took time to straighten my self-valuation. Later, I slowly learned that most of us are average people. That's how the word came about. The measure of a person's worth isn't from the wealth or beauty, but it's what's in the heart. I believe there is goodness and grace and kindness in a lot of people. But you wouldn't know it from their appearance.

In my book, I wanted to show ordinary people, living in ordinary houses, eating ordinary food, sharing ordinary lives, driving ordinary cars. I wanted to show that some people struggle much harder than others, but from those things come people who are kind and loving, care deeply, who think of the needs of others as more important than their own (Philippians 2:1-4). Those people are amazing, and they bring with them the life-energy that spreads to others who are around them.

I also wanted my book to remind me—and you, my friend—that many times we don't get what we pray for. In fact, some prayers feel like they are answered with a stern 'No'. We might feel like God either didn't hear us, He didn't see our need the way we thought He should, or even worse—we consider that God may have ignored us. Because of that, we might think He doesn't care that we struggle to keep our heads above the waters of sadness and grief. As Michael would say in this book "But it's still important to pray." We know that often we pray for someone who is sick, and then death, unforeseen, enters instead of the healing and recovery we hoped would come.

I believe from scripture that God hears our prayers. It's kind of an odd thing we do—stopping to talk to someone we can't see, sometimes with our hands folded, on our knees, or with our eyes closed. But it is our faith in Him that causes us to still go to Him in prayer, even though we have to know in that same faith that we might not see the answer we want. Or we may not see that answer for years.

I have a very distinct memory of praying with a family whose father and husband had just been loaded onto an ambulance in front of their house. I saw the ambulance and because I knew them, I stopped. He was conscious, talking, and I remember praying as though I somehow knew he was going to be just fine. A couple of days later, I was asked to officiate his funeral. I did my best to put together a message of hope and love, but believe me - I stood at that pulpit with the sensation that the rug of my prayer life had been pulled from under me. Understanding was a long distance beyond my horizon.

In my book, I referred to an old hymn called "Farther Along" which states that things may happen and we may lose loved ones, our houses will feel empty and lonely, and we won't be able to understand why in this lifetime. But in the scope of eternity, not only will understanding come, but joy will come with it. I keep the small flyer from his funeral inside my Bible as a reminder that God has His timing, and that I should never get too comfortable with the notion that the gift of another day is just around the corner. It might not be there. (2 Peter 3:10-12)

God may not give us an answer, or he may give us an answer that is opposite of what we expect. But it's still important to pray. That's our connection to an almighty and powerful God.

Spoiler Alert!

So, about this story. You might be asking me if I believe these things to be true and the stats not really in anyone's favor, why did I write a happy reunion into this book? There were versions of this story that did not end happily, and some that didn't leave anyone with a 'warm-fuzzy'. I want you to know that I struggled with the events of this book, because while much of it is fiction, many pieces of it are very close to my life. I won't tell you which, but just for you to understand the way I came to cope with this is to honor those who lifted me, thank those who pointed the way from their own lives, and to create an ending where patience, honesty, doing the right things could rest peacefully with me.
I wanted a book I could hold up and say that I wrote it, and that I'm proud of the story it conveys.

People touch our lives in different ways. They say one thing that encourages us, or they give us a sheet of paper with a hand-drawn schematic that fascinates us at a young age, or they give us train-whistle codes, or that one piece of sage advice that we somehow manage never to forget. I wanted to honor that. To lift it high as an example of what we should be to those who are young and looking for direction. I wanted to honor the feeling of love at a young age and to remember how powerfully it forms us and makes us dream of what we wish for in life. It teaches us how to treat others with respect and dignity, and to see the very best in those we are privileged to live close to.

San Felipe Creek was quite literally one alley-way from behind the house where I lived in Del Rio during my Junior High years. I spent many hours there, swimming and wading in the heat of summer days, walking along in my coat on cold November's day, skimming rocks along the surface, building and bombing wooden boats, other times sitting on the rock wall high over the channel below me, dreaming and thinking, planning and hoping for wonderful things in life. It was a magical place to me, and a magical time to be there. I had many good friends from school and band, several of whom I keep in touch with even today. My model USS Enterprise hung from the ceiling in my room, spinning slowly with the breeze that came through the windows. Many of those things did not survive the move from there, and losing them only added to my sensation of being in a strange world where I knew nobody, where there was no creek or any nearby water. The adjustment caused me to come out of my shell and to give real effort to my life, to make those new friends, to find places I could enjoy, and to even spark those feelings once again that I never thought I would find. Well, believe me, I found them. And I lost a few times, as we all do. But in time, things followed God's path and fell into place the way He planned for them to.

The Ending

The ending of San Felipe Creek wasn't always the ending you read today.

In an earlier version, I thought about how most first loves don't end together. Michael and Trish never found each other again. Michael eventually married, raised a family, and returned to San Felipe Creek years later, where memories of Trish came flooding back.
To add to the solemn feel of that ending, Michael and Ginger's parents had both passed away by the time we got to visiting the place marked by the little lamb. There were others there, Ginger was elsewhere and Michael visited them alone.
My wife, Teresa, had a single sentence in regard to a ceremony where two important people were missing from their front row: It was sad. In fact, everything happening to Michael was sad toward the end, and even at that stage of writing the book, I felt sad, and I couldn't explain why.
When I read that version, I realized something important: I had written an ending that felt plausible, but it wasn't my story. It was never my purpose to take my memories of living in Del Rio and turn them into a huge downer for my readers. That just didn't seem right to me.

I didn't want Michael's memories of Trish—a picture, a few pieces of paper, and a short echo of her voice on a tape—to be all he had left. I wanted the story to find its way back to the creek—and to them.

So I went back and changed it. The result is what you have in your hands.

Steve Martin wrote this line into his screenplay LA Story, "Forget for this moment the smog and the cars and the restaurant and the skating and remember only this. A kiss may not be the truth, but it is what we wish were true."

So, my book may have a fairy-tale ending. But I think it is a reminder of what we can all feel today, if we will just look with appreciation and love at the people God has placed in our lives and the many gifts and blessings He has given us.

These are the good old days.

Give love.

Joel
